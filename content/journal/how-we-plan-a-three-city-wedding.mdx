Three cities in one weekend sounds like a scheduling problem. It's really a trust
problem — you can't be in three places at once, so the plan has to survive being
wrong about something without the couple ever finding out.

## Start with the failure modes

Before we book a single vendor, we ask what happens if a flight is delayed, if a
truck gets stuck in traffic, if a venue's power goes out an hour before doors. Not
because we expect the worst, but because a plan that only works when everything
goes right isn't really a plan.

For a three-city wedding, that means a duplicate vendor bench in every city —
never a single point of failure for anything load-bearing — and a traveling
production lead whose only job is continuity across all three days.

> The couple's only job all weekend was to show up. Everything else moved around
> them.

## Where we simplify

The instinct on a multi-city event is to add more — more staff, more contingency
meetings, more check-ins. We've found the opposite works better: fewer decisions
made in the moment, more decided in advance. A run-of-show that's specific down to
the minute means nobody's improvising when it actually matters.

## What we'd tell you if you're planning one

Pick a lead city and treat the other two as satellites, not equals. Build in a
full buffer day between cities if your guest list can absorb the extra time. And
hire a production lead who has done this before — multi-city logistics are not the
place to learn on the job.
